Some of the finest Georgian and Regency architecture in the country sits in two neighbourhoods either side of Bath's centre: [Lansdown](/bath/lansdown/), climbing the hill above Royal Victoria Park, and [Bathwick](/bath/bathwick/), taking in Great Pulteney Street and the elegant terraces east of Pulteney Bridge. Renovating a property in either isn't like renovating anywhere else in the city. The planning environment is more exacting, the expectations are higher, and the margin for error on materials and detailing is close to zero.

## Why These Two Neighbourhoods Are Different

Lansdown and Bathwick both sit within some of Bath's most protected conservation designations — Bathwick within the boundary of Bath's UNESCO World Heritage Site, Lansdown home to sweeping Regency terraces like Lansdown Crescent and landmarks like Beckford's Tower on the hill above. That level of protection means two things in practice: a higher proportion of properties are Listed, and the conservation officers reviewing applications here have seen every shortcut before. Work that would pass unnoticed elsewhere in Bath gets scrutinised properly in Lansdown and Bathwick, and rightly so.

## Listed Building Consent, in Practice

If a property is Grade I or Grade II Listed — and a significant number are in both neighbourhoods — almost any change beyond redecoration needs Listed Building consent, separate from standard planning permission. That covers:

- Replacing or repairing windows, even like-for-like
- Internal alterations that affect historic fabric, including some non-structural walls
- Changes to fireplaces, cornicing, and other original features
- External repairs using different materials or methods than the original

The application needs a design-and-access statement and, for larger schemes, sometimes a heritage statement justifying the impact of the works. Getting this right the first time matters — a rejected or incomplete application adds months to a project that a well-prepared one wouldn't lose.

## Lansdown: Elevated Standards to Match Elevated Properties

[Lansdown](/bath/lansdown/) is Bath at its most architecturally distinguished. Work here is never routine, and it shouldn't be treated that way: correctly specified lime renders and mortars, accurate sash window restoration or replacement to match original profiles, and period-appropriate internal finishes are the baseline expectation, not an upgrade option. Because so many properties here require Listed Building consent, conservation officer liaison needs to happen early — ideally before a full design is finalised, so the scheme isn't redrawn halfway through the application process.

## Bathwick: Working Within a World Heritage Site

[Bathwick](/bath/bathwick/) carries an additional layer most of Bath doesn't: World Heritage Site status. That doesn't mean internal modernisation is off the table — kitchens, bathrooms, and living spaces get updated here regularly — but it does mean external changes are reviewed against a stricter standard, and estate lease considerations sometimes apply on top of standard planning constraints. The properties around Great Pulteney Street and the Holburne Museum setting are some of the most photographed in the city; conservation officers treat proposals here accordingly.

## What a Realistic Project Timeline Looks Like

For a Listed property in either neighbourhood, budget for:

1. **Pre-application advice** from the conservation team — not mandatory everywhere, but worthwhile on anything beyond minor repairs.
2. **Full Listed Building consent application**, typically 8 weeks for a decision, longer if amendments are requested.
3. **Material sourcing** — genuine Bath stone, appropriate lime products, and matched joinery often have longer lead times than standard building materials.
4. **The build itself**, carried out to a standard that won't need revisiting in five years because a shortcut was taken somewhere.
