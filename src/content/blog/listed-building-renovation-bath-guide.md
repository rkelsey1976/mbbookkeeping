Bath has more listed buildings per square mile than almost any other city in England. The city centre, Bathwick, Lansdown, Larkhall, Widcombe and parts of Bear Flat are all within conservation areas, and the central zone sits within the Bath World Heritage Site buffer zone. If you own a property in Bath, there is a real chance it is listed, or in a conservation area, or both — and what you can and cannot do to the property changes significantly once either of those apply.

This guide explains what the categories mean, what you can and cannot do without consent, and how to plan a sympathetic renovation that satisfies the conservation officer without breaking the budget.

## What Counts as a Listed Building in Bath

A listed building is a building that has been placed on the Statutory List of Buildings of Special Architectural or Historic Interest by Historic England. There are three grades:

- **Grade I** — buildings of exceptional interest (in Bath, this is largely the Georgian public architecture like the Royal Crescent and the Circus). About 2% of listed buildings.
- **Grade II*** — particularly important buildings of more than special interest. About 5.8% of listed buildings.
- **Grade II** — buildings of special interest that warrant preservation. About 91.7% of listed buildings.

The vast majority of listed buildings in Bath are Grade II. The list includes thousands of Georgian and Victorian terraced houses across the city. You can check whether your property is listed at the Historic England website — it takes 30 seconds and the answer is definitive.

## What a Listing Means for Renovation Work

Listed building consent is required for any work that affects the special architectural or historic interest of the building. This includes:

- External alterations (replacing windows, doors, roof coverings, chimneys, render)
- Internal alterations that affect character (removing fireplaces, altering staircases, removing cornicing, changing floor plans)
- Demolition of any part of the building
- Work to boundary walls, gates, railings and outbuildings that are within the curtilage of the listing

Listed building consent is a separate application from planning permission. You may need both. A common mistake is to assume that because planning permission is not required, listed building consent is not required either. This is not the case. Both are independent and both must be in place before work starts.

## What You Can Do Without Listed Building Consent

Some minor work does not require listed building consent. The general rule is that like-for-like repairs using matching materials do not require consent. Examples:

- Re-pointing using the same mortar (in Bath, this almost always means lime mortar, not cement)
- Replacing a broken slate with a matching slate
- Painting using the same colour and type of paint
- Repairing an existing window with matching timber
- Like-for-like replacement of roof tiles

But — and this is important — even "like-for-like" repairs can require consent if they affect the special interest of the building. The safest course is to contact the local authority conservation officer before starting any work, even on what you think is routine maintenance. A 5-minute phone call can save a retrospective consent application, which is more expensive and less likely to be approved.

## What You Cannot Do Without Consent

Some work is almost never acceptable on a listed building without significant justification:

- Removing or altering Bath stone features (surrounds, cornicing, quoins, walling)
- Replacing original timber sash windows with UPVC
- Using cement-based mortar on a building originally constructed with lime mortar
- Removing internal panelling, staircases, fireplaces or other original features
- Cladding or rendering a Bath stone facade
- Installing roof lights on a front-facing roof slope in a sensitive location
- Altering the height or profile of a chimney
- Removing or significantly altering a boundary wall

For each of these, you may be able to get consent, but you will need a heritage statement, often a structural report, and the work will be carefully scrutinised by the conservation officer. The cost of getting the application right is small; the cost of doing the work and then having to undo it is enormous.

## What is Different About Bath Specifically

Bath is unusual among English cities in that a large proportion of the city is built from a single material — Bath stone (a type of oolitic limestone quarried locally). The World Heritage Site status, granted in 1987, gives Bath's conservation regime more weight than the average English town.

**Conservation areas in Bath** are extensive. Almost the entire city centre is in a conservation area, and many of the surrounding Victorian and Edwardian suburbs are too. In a conservation area, you need conservation area consent for demolition of any structure over 115 cubic metres, and you need planning permission for work that would not normally require it (such as cladding, some extensions, and some alterations to roofs).

**The Bath Preservation Trust** is a statutory consultee on planning applications in Bath. They are well-resourced, well-organised, and review applications carefully. They have a strong track record of objecting to applications they consider harmful to Bath's heritage.

**Article 4 Directions** are in place across much of Bath. These remove permitted development rights that would otherwise apply, meaning that many small works (such as replacing windows, changing external paint colours, or installing satellite dishes) require planning permission or conservation area consent in addition to any other consent.

## How to Plan a Listed Building Renovation in Bath

**1. Get a heritage statement.** This is a document that sets out the historic significance of the building, the proposed works, and why the works are appropriate. For a straightforward internal alteration, a short heritage statement may suffice. For a major renovation or extension, you will need a full heritage statement prepared by an architect with conservation experience.

**2. Talk to the conservation officer early.** Bath & North East Somerset Council has a planning duty officer available by phone, and conservation officers will usually respond to email enquiries about specific proposals. A pre-application conversation can save weeks of formal application time and identify issues before you commit to a design.

**3. Choose materials that match the original.** Bath stone for stonework, lime mortar for pointing, timber for windows and doors, slate for roofing. The conservation officer will not accept a proposal that uses cement where lime is original, or UPVC where timber is original.

**4. Use a builder with heritage experience.** A builder who has not worked on listed buildings before will not understand the differences in technique. Lime mortar needs to be mixed and applied differently to cement. Bath stone repairs need to use matching stone. Timber window repairs need to use traditional joinery techniques. A generalist builder can do more harm than good on a listed building.

## Common Listed Building Mistakes in Bath

**Painting over Bath stone.** A frequent mistake. Bath stone is a porous material that needs to breathe. Painting or rendering it traps moisture, leading to damp and structural damage. The stone is one of the building's most valued features — it should be left visible.

**Cement re-pointing.** Equally common, equally damaging. Modern cement mortar is harder and less porous than the original lime mortar. When applied to a building constructed with lime, it traps moisture, prevents the wall from breathing, and causes the stone faces to spall. The repair becomes worse than the original problem.

**Replacing sash windows with UPVC.** Even if the original windows are draughty, the conservation officer will almost never accept UPVC replacements on a listed building. Secondary glazing, or careful repair of the original timber sashes, is the correct approach.

**Removing internal features.** Buyers sometimes rip out original panelling, fireplaces, cornicing and built-in cupboards because they want a "modern look". These features are often protected, and removing them without consent is a criminal offence. Even if consent is given, the cost of removal is rarely recovered on resale.

## What a Bath Heritage Renovation Costs

Listed building renovations in Bath are typically 10-25% more expensive than the equivalent work on an unlisted property. The additional cost comes from:

- Specialist materials (Bath stone, lime mortar, traditional timber, slate)
- More careful working methods (hand-mixing lime mortar, slower stonework)
- Heritage statement and conservation architect fees if needed
- Longer programme (more careful sequencing, more drying time for lime-based materials)

The benefit is that a well-restored listed Bath property holds its value significantly better than the average UK property, and may qualify for grants or tax relief in some circumstances.
