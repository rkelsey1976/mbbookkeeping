If you're wondering whether a few rooflights will do the job, or whether you need a full dormer, you're asking the right question. For most Bath homeowners, the answer depends less on budget than on the shape of the roof, the planning position of the property, and what the room will actually be used for.

Here is an honest, experience-based comparison of Velux and dormer loft conversions in Bath, written for people who want to make a decision rather than collect more options.

## The Short Answer

A **Velux conversion** keeps the existing roofline and adds rooflights. It's cheaper, faster, and often doesn't need planning permission. The trade-off is usable space limited by the existing roof shape and head height.

A **dormer conversion** extends the roof vertically to create a box-shape addition, dramatically increasing head height and floor area. It costs more and almost always needs planning permission — but for most Bath Victorian and Edwardian terraces, it's the option that genuinely delivers a bedroom or master suite rather than a storage compromise. Both fall under what we'd generally call [our loft conversion service](/bath-loft-conversion/) — the difference is purely the degree of structural alteration.

## Velux Conversions

A Velux (or "rooflight") conversion is the simplest form of loft conversion. The roof structure stays as it is. The work is:

- Strengthen and insulate the floor
- Board out and plaster the loft
- Install a staircase from the floor below
- Fit Velux (or equivalent) roof windows
- Add electrics, heating and decoration

No change to the roofline, no structural alteration to the outside of the property.

### Where Velux Works Well

**Storage-to-bedroom conversions.** A basic extra bedroom, home office or nursery often does the job at the lowest possible cost.

**Period properties with steep roof pitches.** Victorian terraces in Oldfield Park, Bear Flat and Twerton frequently have steeply pitched roofs with usable ridge heights of 2.4m or more. A Velux conversion here feels surprisingly spacious without changing the roofline.

### Where Velux Falls Down

**Limited head height in shallow roofs.** Building regulations require at least 2.2m of head height over at least 50% of the floor area. If your loft doesn't have this — common in Georgian townhouses with shallow pitches or in bungalows — a Velux conversion either isn't viable, or produces a non-compliant space without significant structural work.

**Restricted layout.** The usable space is dictated by the eaves. You typically lose 1m to 1.5m of width along each side where the roof slopes down. Furnishing around sloping ceilings is unavoidable.

## Dormer Conversions

A dormer extends a section of the existing roof vertically, creating a flat-roofed (or slightly pitched) box that pushes out from the slope. The internal effect is dramatic — full head height across most of the floor, walls where the slope used to be, and a room that feels like a proper first-floor extension rather than a loft.

### Where Dormer Works Well

**Tight head height.** Where shallow pitch rules out a Velux, a dormer creates the head height the original roof doesn't have.

**Maximum usable floor area.** A rear dormer on a Victorian terrace typically converts the entire loft into well-proportioned space — large enough for a bedroom with ensuite, or two smaller bedrooms and a bathroom.

**Better for en-suites and stairs.** Dormers create proper wall space, making it straightforward to fit a bathroom or larger staircase. In a Velux, the staircase alone can eat a disproportionate amount of floor area.

### Where Dormer Adds Cost or Complexity

**Planning permission.** Most Bath properties sit within conservation areas; many are within the World Heritage Site buffer zone; many are also listed. Permitted development rights under Class B of the General Permitted Development Order are restricted or removed entirely in conservation areas.

Almost every rear dormer in central Bath requires a full planning application. Front dormers are more contentious and frequently refused in conservation areas because they change the streetscape. On a listed building, you'll need listed building consent as well.

**Cost and duration.** A dormer is roughly double the cost of a Velux, and the project typically takes 8–12 weeks rather than 4–6, with scaffolding, structural alteration and a planning application adding to the timeline and price.

## Bath-Specific Planning Realities

Bath is one of the most planning-constrained places to do a loft conversion in England. Almost all of central Bath — Widcombe, Larkhall, Bathwick, Lansdown, Walcot, the city centre — sits within a conservation area. Permitted development rights for rear dormers are restricted, and you'll need a planning application even for what feels like a modest alteration.

Several Bath wards have Article 4 Directions removing permitted development entirely. Bath has over 5,000 listed buildings; if yours is one, internal roof alterations usually require listed building consent even where planning permission isn't needed. The UNESCO World Heritage designation covers a large area around the city centre, and planners apply particular scrutiny to work visible from public viewpoints.

In practice, this favours a smaller Velux conversion or a careful rear dormer over a large front dormer. A pre-application enquiry to B&NES planning before commissioning drawings will save significant cost and time — and will tell you, fairly definitively, which option has a realistic chance of approval.

## My Honest Decision Framework

**Choose Velux when:** head height is already adequate (2.2m+); you're converting from storage to occasional use — guest room, hobby space, home office; the property is in a sensitive planning location where a dormer is unlikely to be approved; or budget is the deciding factor.

**Choose dormer when:** the roof pitch is shallow and head height is the limiting factor; you genuinely need a bedroom, master suite or larger living space; you're staying in the property long enough to justify the investment; a well-designed rear dormer is likely to be approved; or layout matters — you need a usable staircase and room for a bed, wardrobes and an ensuite.

Velux conversions in Bath typically run £18,000 to £28,000; rear dormers £35,000 to £55,000 — the [full loft conversion cost guide](/blog/loft-conversion-cost-bath-2026/) breaks down where those figures move. Mansard conversions on Georgian and Regency properties — a different beast — start at £50,000. Planning fees, structural engineer reports and party wall agreements typically add £1,500 to £4,000, and these aren't optional.
